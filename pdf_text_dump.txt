1. Context & Background 
Jyot builds AI-driven products spanning intelligent document/OCR pipelines, media processing systems, 
and real-time communication tooling. An AI Generalist here is expected to move across all three: 
reading messy real-world documents and images, reasoning over them with LLMs, and wrapping the 
result in a reliable, observable, safe system. 
Because the role spans agentic AI and document intelligence, we have extended the assignment in two 
ways: (1) the content you ingest includes images and scanned documents, so OCR is a first-class 
component, and (2) the processing core must be a genuine agent that decides what to do per item, 
rather than a fixed script that calls an LLM at the end. No knowledge of our internal codebase, data, or 
domain is required. 
2. How This Assignment Works 
There is a single assignment for you. You are free to choose the source, topic, models, and frameworks, 
and every choice you make should be justified. We would rather see a smaller scope executed 
rigorously, with honest measurement, than a broad scope that only works on the happy path. 
Please note that we place heavy weight on evidence. Claims such as "the agent handles prompt 
injection" or "OCR is accurate" must be backed by tests and numbers produced by your own evaluation 
harness (Section 6).

3. Objective 
Build an agentic pipeline that repeatedly ingests posts (text and image/PDF attachments) from public 
sources, extracts text from visual content using OCR, classifies and deduplicates items (including cross-
modal duplicates, such as the same announcement posted once as text and once as a screenshot), 
scores relevance and urgency, and produces a prioritised, evidence-linked digest, with a human-
approval-gated alert for high-priority items. 
The system must be safe against hostile content (including instructions hidden inside images), bounded 
in cost, resumable after failure, and measurable. 
The objective in plain terms 
Imagine a small team that must stay on top of one online topic (for example, product-recall notices) but 
cannot read hundreds of posts a day, many of them screenshots or scanned notices. You are building 
the assistant that does the reading for them: it collects new posts, reads the text inside images, merges 
duplicates, decides how much each story matters, and hands the team a short ranked digest. If 
something looks urgent, it drafts an alert, but a human must approve it before anything is sent. 
We are less interested in the digest itself than in how the system decides, protects itself, and proves it 
works. In short, we are testing three things: 
1. Real agent decisions: the system chooses which tool to use per item, when to retry with 
another approach, and when to stop, within a fixed budget. 
2. Safe and reliable behaviour on bad input: blurry or rotated images, instructions hidden inside 
images, rate limits, crashes, and malformed files. 
3. Honest measurement: numbers for OCR accuracy, classification, deduplication, and attack 
resistance, including where the system fails. 
End-to-end flow 
Every item enters the same loop, but the agent decides how far each one goes. A plain text post may 
finish in one step; a blurry screenshot may trigger a second OCR engine; an urgent claim may trigger 
verification before an alert is proposed.

4. Task Description 
Stage 1: Ingestion (repeatable, replayable) 
• Choose at least two public, authentication-light sources, of which at least one must regularly 
contain image or PDF attachments (examples: Reddit image posts via the public JSON endpoints, 
Mastodon public hashtag timelines, RSS feeds with enclosures, Wikimedia Commons, public 
government or notice-board RSS feeds). Pick a topic scope of your choice (for example: product 
recalls and safety notices, outage reports, scholarship/job notices, local civic alerts). 
• The ingester must be runnable repeatedly (poll or scheduled) and fetch only new items (track 
cursors or seen IDs persistently). 
• Replay mode is mandatory. Implement --replay <dir>, which runs the whole pipeline on 
recorded fixtures (JSON + image/PDF files) with no network access. Evaluators will run your 
system on a held-out fixture pack in this mode. 
Stage 2: OCR and visual extraction 
• Implement OCR for image attachments and for scanned/image-only PDF pages. 
• Use at least two extraction engines and a router that chooses between them. For example: a 
classical OCR engine (Tesseract, PaddleOCR, EasyOCR, docTR) and a vision-capable LLM. The 
router must make an explicit, logged decision based on signals such as image quality, OCR 
confidence, text density, script/language, or layout type. 
• Include a preprocessing stage appropriate to the content (for example: deskew/rotation 
detection, denoise, binarisation, resolution upscaling, cropping of screenshot chrome). Show 
with measurements that preprocessing helps (or does not).

• Handle at least: (a) screenshots of text, (b) photographed/rotated or low-contrast images, (c) a 
scanned PDF with more than one page, and (d) one non-English or non-Latin script of your 
choice (for example Hindi or Gujarati), alongside English. 
• For at least one document type that has structure (for example a notice, receipt, table, or form), 
extract structured fields into a JSON schema with validation, and flag fields the model could not 
read confidently instead of guessing. 
• Every OCR result must carry: engine used, confidence (calibrated or at least documented), 
language, and a flag for "needs second opinion". 
Stage 3: The agent (the core of the assignment) 
Build an agent loop in which a planner/controller (LLM-driven) decides, per item, which tools to call, in 
what order, and when to stop. A fixed linear chain of fixed steps does not satisfy this stage.

Requirements: 
• Bounded autonomy: a maximum step count per item, a per-item and per-run token/cost 
budget, and graceful degradation when a budget is hit (for example: lower-cost path, mark item 
"partially processed"). 
• Variable trajectories: your logs must demonstrate at least three clearly different trajectories on 
real inputs (for example: text-only post resolved in one step; blurry screenshot that triggers re-
OCR with a different engine; urgent claim that triggers verify_claim). 
• Structured outputs: all LLM outputs are schema-validated (for example with Pydantic), with 
bounded retries and a deterministic fallback on repeated failure. 
• Self-checking: for items scored above your urgency threshold, add a critique, self-consistency, 
or verifier step, and show whether it changed outcomes.

• Full tracing: every run writes a JSONL trace (item ID, step, tool, arguments, result summary, 
tokens, latency, cost, decision rationale) that a reviewer can replay. 
• Framework choice is free (LangGraph, a custom loop, or another approach). If you use a 
framework, state what it gave you; if you hand-roll the loop, state why. 
Stage 4: Classification, scoring, and deduplication 
• Tag each item by subtopic (taxonomy of your design; document it). 
• Score relevance and urgency with an LLM/prompt-based approach, and include at least one 
non-LLM signal in the final score (for example recency, source credibility, engagement velocity, 
corroboration count). Explain the combination logic. 
• Deduplicate across modalities and phrasing: near-identical text posts, reposts, and the same 
content as image vs text. Use at least two techniques (for example embeddings + MinHash for 
text, perceptual hash for images, OCR-text embedding for cross-modal matches). Group related 
items into story clusters rather than merely dropping duplicates, and keep the best-quality 
representative. 
• Maintain persistent memory across runs so that the next digest can state what is new, what 
escalated, and what was already reported. 
Stage 5: Safety and adversarial robustness 
All content, including text recovered from images, is untrusted data. Implement and test: 
• Prompt injection via OCR: images containing text such as instructions to the AI, to raise its own 
score, or to call tools. The agent must not follow them, and your tests must show it. Describe 
your isolation strategy (for example: separating data from instructions, restricting tool 
permissions while processing untrusted content, output validation). 
• PII handling: detect and redact emails, phone numbers, and similar identifiers recovered from 
screenshots before they reach the digest or alerts. 
• Network safety: allow-listed schemes, blocked private IP ranges, response size limits, content-
type verification. 
• Spam/manipulation: a defence against low-quality, repetitive, or coordinated posts (state the 
approach and its limits). 
Stage 6: Reliability and cost control 
• Handle rate limits (backoff with jitter, respect Retry-After), timeouts, malformed files, and 
model errors with graceful fallbacks and logging. 
• Runs must be idempotent and resumable: killing the process mid-run and restarting must not 
duplicate work or digest entries. 
• Cache OCR and LLM results by content hash. 
• Implement a --fault-injection flag that randomly or deterministically simulates failures 
(HTTP 429/500, corrupted image, OCR engine crash, LLM returning invalid JSON) so that 
robustness can be demonstrated.

Stage 7: Human-in-the-loop alerting 
• The agent may only propose alerts. Proposals are written to an approval queue (a file or simple 
CLI/web view is sufficient). A human approves or rejects, and only then is the alert emitted 
(console, webhook stub, or file write; no real SMS/WhatsApp needed). 
• Each proposal must include the evidence: source link, extracted text snippet or image reference, 
confidence, verification result, and why the urgency threshold was met. 
Stage 8: Digest 
• Generate a prioritised top-N digest (Markdown, HTML, or PDF) grouped by story cluster, 
including: score breakdown, source links, evidence snippets from OCR where relevant 
(redacted), flags such as "low OCR confidence" or "unverified", and a "what changed since last 
digest" section.

11. Tips & Guidance for Candidates 
• Think system-first, code-second. Decide the agent's tool contract and the trace format before 
writing prompts. 
• Prioritise robustness over completeness. A smaller scope that degrades gracefully beats a brittle 
system that attempts everything. 
• Measure before you claim. If something does not help (for example a preprocessing step, or the 
agent loop on simple items), say so; this is a positive signal. 
• Treat OCR output as noisy and as hostile. Never let recovered text act as instructions. 
• Keep cost visible. Know what a digest costs and where the money goes. 
• Document your reasoning, not just your code. We care as much about how you think through 
trade-offs as about the final output.
